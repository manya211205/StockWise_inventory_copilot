# Swiggy Instamart: Product Teardown and Margin Improvement Strategy

## 1. Executive Summary

### The Business Problem

Swiggy Instamart operates in India's competitive quick-commerce market, where rapid delivery, product availability, customer acquisition, and fulfilment costs must be balanced against sustainable unit economics.

Despite growth in orders and net order value, Instamart has continued to report substantial adjusted EBITDA losses. The central challenge is to improve order-level and store-level economics without compromising the convenience and reliability that attract customers.

**Central question:** How can Swiggy Instamart improve contribution per order, increase dark-store productivity, and build a sustainable path to profitability without damaging customer retention?

### Key Findings

- **Growth does not guarantee profitability.** Higher order volumes may increase losses if incremental orders do not generate sufficient contribution.
- **Unit economics need to be diagnosed first.** Revenue, product margins, promotions, and variable fulfilment costs must be evaluated together.
- **Dark-store productivity matters.** Store utilization, demand density, operating expenses, and inventory availability can influence profitability.
- **Promotions should be evaluated on incrementality.** Discounts are valuable when the additional contribution they generate justifies their cost.
- **Customer experience must remain a guardrail.** Cost reductions that damage reliability, availability, or retention may undermine long-term growth.

### Recommended Approach

This teardown recommends a data-led margin improvement strategy:

1. Diagnose contribution per order and store-level economics.
2. Identify the most material and actionable source of margin pressure using internal data.
3. Test targeted promotion optimization through a controlled experiment.
4. Evaluate improvements in basket contribution, inventory availability, and fulfilment efficiency.
5. Scale interventions only when they demonstrate measurable financial benefits without unacceptable customer-experience trade-offs.

## 2. Business Context and Latest Reported Performance

### 2.1 Q2 FY27 Financial Snapshot

The following figures were reported for the quarter ended September 30, 2026.

| Metric | Swiggy Instamart | Blinkit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Adjusted EBITDA | -₹895 crore | +₹184 crore |
| Dark stores at quarter-end | 1,240 | 2,751 |
| Net order value (NOV) | ₹6,594 crore | ₹20,940 crore |
| Year-on-year NOV growth | 34.8% | 79.3% |

Sources: Mint, Reuters, and NDTV Profit. See Sources and References.

**Metric clarification:** Adjusted EBITDA is a measure of operating performance before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, subject to company-specific adjustments. It is not equivalent to net profit or cash flow.

NOV refers to net order value under the respective company's reporting definition. It should not be confused with revenue or profit.

### 2.2 What the Numbers Suggest

**Observation 1: Instamart is growing, but its losses remain substantial.**

Instamart's NOV reportedly grew 34.8% year-on-year to ₹6,594 crore. Orders increased 29.3% to approximately 130 million, while monthly transacting users increased 23% to 14.8 million.

This indicates that the business is attracting customers and processing more orders, but growth has not yet translated into adjusted EBITDA profitability.

**Observation 2: Blinkit operates at a substantially larger scale.**

Blinkit's larger store network, higher NOV, and faster growth may support greater purchasing leverage, denser delivery operations, and stronger utilization of fixed costs.

However, store count alone does not explain the difference in reported performance. Differences in average order value, contribution per order, operating costs, store maturity, product mix, and business models may also matter.

**Observation 3: Growth needs to be supported by attractive unit economics.**

Increasing order volume can help absorb fixed costs, but only when incremental orders generate sufficient contribution. If incremental orders have negative contribution after variable costs, growth may increase losses rather than resolve them.

**Key takeaway:** The business problem is not simply how to increase orders or dark stores. It is how to grow while improving the economics of each order and each store.

## 3. Problem Statement and Business Objective

### 3.1 Primary Problem Statement

Swiggy Instamart has demonstrated meaningful customer and order growth, yet its quick-commerce business continues to generate substantial adjusted EBITDA losses.

The business needs to improve the economics of individual orders and dark stores while preserving the customer value proposition that drives purchase frequency and retention.

### 3.2 Business Objective

Improve Instamart's contribution per order and store-level operating performance, thereby reducing adjusted EBITDA losses and establishing a credible path toward sustainable profitability.

### 3.3 Constraints

The strategy should avoid:

- Reducing discounts indiscriminately if doing so materially harms customer retention.
- Increasing basket size through unnecessary purchases that damage customer trust.
- Expanding into locations without sufficient demand density.
- Cutting delivery or store costs in ways that materially reduce reliability.
- Expanding assortment or private labels without validating demand, margin, and inventory risks.

The goal is profitable, durable growth—not profitability achieved at the expense of the core customer experience.

## 4. Root-Cause Analysis: Problems to Investigate

The following are potential causes of weak unit economics. Public disclosures provide context for the analysis, but they do not establish the precise contribution of each cause to Instamart's losses.

### Problem 1: Low Contribution per Order

**Hypothesis:** Revenue generated per order may not sufficiently cover variable fulfilment, delivery, discounting, payment, and other order-related costs.

Swiggy has discussed improving revenue per order and reducing cost per order in its financial communications. This indicates that order economics are a relevant area of focus, although the latest quarter's detailed unit economics must be assessed separately.

**Data required:**
- Revenue per order.
- Discounts and promotional cost per order.
- Picking, packing, and delivery cost per order.
- Contribution margin by city, store, basket size, and customer cohort.

**Why it matters:** Without a consistent contribution-per-order calculation, the company cannot reliably determine whether growth in a particular segment is creating or destroying value.

### Problem 2: Uneven Dark-Store Productivity

**Hypothesis:** Some stores may not generate sufficient orders or gross profit to cover their operating costs.

A larger network can improve availability and coverage, but it also creates rent, labor, inventory, and other fixed or semi-fixed costs.

**Data required:**
- Orders per store per day.
- Gross profit and contribution per store.
- Store-level operating expenses.
- Time taken for new stores to reach stable order volume.
- Store-level profitability distribution.

The appropriate response is not automatically to open more stores or close existing ones. It is to identify which locations have attractive demand and which require operational improvement.

### Problem 3: Demand Density and Delivery Efficiency

**Hypothesis:** In some service areas, order density may be insufficient to support efficient delivery operations.

Low density can increase travel time per order, reduce orders completed per delivery-partner hour, and increase fulfilment cost.

**Data required:**
- Orders by delivery zone and time of day.
- Delivery cost per completed order.
- Orders per delivery-partner hour.
- Average delivery distance and time.
- Cancellation and late-delivery rates.

### Problem 4: Customer Acquisition and Promotional Spending

**Hypothesis:** Some customers may be disproportionately dependent on discounts, making their orders less profitable.

Promotions can attract customers and increase purchase frequency. However, discounts that subsidize purchases that would have occurred anyway may reduce margin without creating incremental value.

**Data required:**
- Discount cost per order and customer.
- Incremental orders attributable to promotions.
- Repeat purchase and retention by acquisition cohort.
- Contribution margin for discounted versus non-discounted orders.
- Customer lifetime contribution after acquisition costs.

### Problem 5: Basket Economics and Assortment

**Hypothesis:** A subset of baskets may be too small or have an unfavorable product mix to cover fulfilment costs.

Swiggy has discussed differentiated assortment, including its Noice private label, Nectr fresh produce offering, and Switch brand partnerships.

Assortment expansion can create revenue opportunities, but low-velocity products may increase inventory holding costs, spoilage, and working-capital requirements.

**Data required:**
- Average order value and items per order.
- Gross margin by category and SKU.
- Inventory turnover and stockout rates.
- Waste, expiry, and shrinkage.
- Incremental basket contribution from cross-category purchases.

### Problem 6: Inventory Ownership and Working Capital

**Hypothesis:** The operating model and inventory-management process may influence gross margin, stock availability, inventory losses, and working-capital efficiency.

Swiggy has discussed an inventory-led operating model for Instamart and indicated that it expects this approach to improve contribution margin when fully implemented.

The actual effect will depend on supplier terms, purchasing efficiency, inventory turns, product freshness, shrinkage, and execution.

**Data required:**
- Gross margin by category.
- Supplier terms and purchase costs.
- Inventory days and stock turnover.
- Waste and shrinkage rates.
- Stock availability and fulfilment rates.
- Working capital tied up in inventory.

### Root-Cause Analysis Summary

These hypotheses are related rather than independent. For example, poor inventory availability can reduce basket completion, while excessive inventory can increase working-capital needs and waste. Similarly, low order density can increase fulfilment costs and weaken store-level contribution.

The first step should therefore be to establish a consistent view of unit economics and identify which factors explain the largest differences between orders, stores, and customer cohorts.

## 5. Recommendations

The recommendations below are proposed interventions to test, not confirmed solutions to Instamart's losses.

### Recommendation 1: Build a Contribution-per-Order Improvement Program

**Priority: Very high**

Create a unified unit-economics view for each order, store, city, and customer cohort.

Track revenue and variable costs consistently so the business can identify where contribution is being lost.

**Actions:**
1. Break down revenue per order into product margin, delivery charges, platform fees, advertising, and other applicable revenue.
2. Track variable costs such as discounts, picking, packing, delivery, payment processing, and product losses.
3. Identify low-contribution order segments.
4. Run targeted interventions against the largest verified cost drivers.
5. Review contribution changes alongside retention and customer satisfaction.

**Why this matters:** It creates a measurable foundation for every other margin-improvement initiative.

### Recommendation 2: Improve Dark-Store Productivity Before Accelerating Expansion

**Priority: Very high**

Evaluate stores by maturity, demand density, contribution, and operating cost.

**Actions:**
- Segment stores into new, ramping, mature-profitable, and underperforming cohorts.
- Identify the reasons behind weak productivity in underperforming stores.
- Improve local assortment and inventory availability where demand supports it.
- Adjust staffing to observed order patterns while protecting service levels.
- Expand selectively in areas where expected demand and store-level economics justify the investment.

**Success measures:**
- Orders per store per day.
- Store-level contribution.
- Time to reach store-level contribution breakeven.
- Rent and labor cost per order.
- Store-level return on invested capital.

**Trade-off:** Slower expansion may sacrifice some market-share growth. However, opening stores without an attractive path to adequate utilization can increase operating losses.

### Recommendation 3: Make Promotions Incremental and Contribution-Aware

**Priority: Very high**

Move from broad discounting toward targeted promotions based on incremental customer value.

**Actions:**
- Test promotions among comparable customer groups.
- Measure incremental orders and contribution rather than total orders alone.
- Reduce subsidies for customers who purchase regularly without discounts, where evidence supports doing so.
- Use targeted offers to reactivate genuinely lapsed customers.
- Avoid abrupt discount withdrawal without measuring its effect on retention.

**Success measures:**
- Incremental contribution per promotion.
- Cost per incremental order.
- Repeat purchase after the promotion.
- Retention by acquisition cohort.
- Share of orders that remain contribution-positive after incentives.

**Trade-off:** Lower promotional spending may improve short-term margins but could reduce acquisition or purchase frequency. Experiments must measure both effects.

### Recommendation 4: Improve Basket Economics Without Compromising Trust

**Priority: High**

Encourage customers to consolidate relevant purchases when doing so improves convenience and order economics.

**Possible experiments:**
- Relevant frequently bought-together recommendations.
- Reminders for commonly replenished household essentials.
- Curated grocery lists based on user-selected needs.
- Contextual suggestions for complementary products.
- Clear communication of minimum-order requirements or delivery charges.

Recommendations should be relevant rather than manipulative. Customers should not be encouraged to buy products they do not need.

**Success measures:**
- Incremental contribution per order.
- Average order value.
- Items per order.
- Conversion rate.
- Repeat purchase and customer complaints.

### Recommendation 5: Optimize Assortment and Inventory Availability Together

**Priority: High**

Use SKU-level demand and profitability data to determine what each store should stock.

**Actions:**
- Identify high-demand, high-contribution products that frequently go out of stock.
- Improve replenishment planning for important essentials.
- Reduce inventory exposure to low-velocity products where demand evidence supports the decision.
- Monitor expiry, spoilage, and shrinkage for relevant categories.
- Test category-specific assortment rather than using the same assortment everywhere.

**Success measures:**
- Product availability rate.
- Stockout rate for priority SKUs.
- Lost sales attributable to stockouts, where measurable.
- Inventory turnover.
- Waste and shrinkage.
- Gross profit after inventory losses.

**Trade-off:** Reducing assortment can lower inventory costs but may also reduce basket completion and customer satisfaction. Assortment changes should be tested at store or category level.

### Recommendation 6: Improve Delivery Density and Fulfilment Efficiency

**Priority: High**

Investigate where delivery costs are elevated relative to order volume.

**Actions:**
- Analyze demand by geographic zone and time of day.
- Improve order allocation and dispatch where operationally feasible.
- Align staffing and delivery capacity with demand patterns.
- Evaluate delivery radii and service promises using actual cost and customer outcomes.
- Avoid expanding coverage in areas where demand cannot support efficient fulfilment.

**Success measures:**
- Delivery cost per order.
- Orders per delivery-partner hour.
- On-time delivery rate.
- Cancellation rate.
- Customer complaints and repeat usage.

**Trade-off:** Narrower coverage or different service promises could reduce costs but may make the service less convenient. Reliability and accessibility should remain guardrails.

### Recommendation 7: Use Private Labels and Differentiated Assortment Selectively

**Priority: Medium–high**

Instamart's existing private-label and differentiated-assortment initiatives provide a starting point for evaluating whether exclusive products can improve margins and customer differentiation.

**Actions:**
- Identify categories with repeat demand and attractive margin potential.
- Test a limited assortment before expanding.
- Compare contribution after sourcing, fulfilment, marketing, and inventory losses.
- Evaluate whether private-label purchases are incremental or replace profitable existing products.
- Monitor quality, complaints, and repeat purchase.

**Success measures:**
- Contribution margin by SKU.
- Repeat purchase rate.
- Inventory turnover.
- Waste and returns.
- Customer satisfaction and complaints.

**Trade-off:** Private labels may offer better product economics, but they introduce quality, brand, and inventory risks. Higher gross margin does not automatically mean higher net contribution.

### Recommendation 8: Evaluate Store-Level Operating-Model Changes

**Priority: Medium–high**

Evaluate the economics of the inventory-led operating model discussed by Swiggy using controlled comparisons where feasible.

**Actions:**
- Compare comparable stores or categories operating under different models.
- Track purchase cost, product availability, inventory turns, and shrinkage.
- Measure changes in gross margin and contribution.
- Monitor working capital and operational complexity.
- Expand the model only when measured benefits exceed the additional costs and risks.

**Success measures:**
- Gross margin.
- Contribution margin.
- Inventory days.
- Stockout and fulfilment rates.
- Waste and shrinkage.
- Working-capital requirements.

**Trade-off:** Greater control over inventory and sourcing may improve economics but can increase inventory risk and working-capital needs.

## 6. Prioritization Framework

The recommendations should be evaluated using the RICE framework:

**RICE Score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort**

The framework helps compare initiatives, but reliable numerical scores require estimates grounded in internal data. This teardown therefore uses a qualitative initial ranking rather than invented RICE scores.

| Initiative | Potential impact | Relative effort | Initial priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution-per-order visibility | High enabling value | Medium | Very high |
| Dark-store productivity | Potentially high | Medium–high | Very high |
| Promotion optimization | Potentially high | Medium | Very high |
| Basket economics | Medium–high | Medium | High |
| Assortment and inventory optimization | Potentially high | Medium–high | High |
| Delivery efficiency | Medium–high | Medium–high | High |
| Private-label expansion | Potentially high | High | Medium–high |
| Operating-model evaluation | Potentially high | High | Medium–high |

These priorities reflect the proposed sequence of investigation and execution, not a measured ranking of financial impact. The order should change if internal data identifies a different dominant source of losses.

### Selected Investigation Priority

**First: Diagnose contribution per order and dark-store productivity.**

These areas provide a foundation for evaluating the economics of Instamart's existing operations.

Before recommending expansion, cost reductions, or promotional changes, the business needs to understand:

- Whether orders generate positive contribution after relevant variable costs.
- Which stores generate sufficient contribution to cover their operating costs.
- Whether new stores follow a viable path toward maturity.
- Which cost or revenue components explain the largest differences between stores and customer segments.

This is an investigation priority, not a conclusion that either factor is the proven primary cause of Instamart's losses.

### Decision Rule

The first operational intervention should target the problem that demonstrates:

1. Material financial impact.
2. Credible evidence of an underlying cause.
3. A feasible intervention.
4. Measurable improvement in contribution or operating costs.
5. Acceptable customer-experience and retention outcomes.

If internal data shows that another area, such as promotions or inventory losses, accounts for a larger share of losses, the priority should change accordingly.

## 7. Metrics Framework

### 7.1 Primary Business Metric

**Contribution per completed order**, evaluated alongside customer retention and service quality.

This is a proposed business-level metric, not necessarily Instamart's official North Star Metric.

Contribution per order helps evaluate whether revenue generated by each order adequately covers its relevant variable costs. It should be interpreted alongside total contribution and the cost of operating the store network.

### 7.2 Supporting Metrics

**Order economics**
- Contribution per order.
- Contribution margin as a percentage of order value.
- Revenue per order.
- Variable cost per order.
- Discount expenditure per order.

**Store economics**
- Orders per store per day.
- Store-level contribution.
- Time to store-level breakeven.
- Rent and labor cost per order.
- Store-level return on invested capital.

**Customer outcomes**
- Monthly transacting users.
- Repeat purchase rate.
- Order frequency.
- Basket completion and conversion.
- Customer retention by acquisition cohort.

**Inventory outcomes**
- Product availability rate.
- Inventory turnover.
- Waste and shrinkage.
- Stockout rate for priority SKUs.

### 7.3 Guardrail Metrics

Any margin-improvement initiative should also monitor:

- Cancellation rate.
- On-time delivery rate.
- Customer complaints and refunds.
- Repeat purchase and retention.
- Product quality and freshness.
- Availability of essential products.

An improvement in margin that causes a material deterioration in these outcomes may not be sustainable.

### 7.4 How to Interpret the Metrics Together

Metrics should not be optimized in isolation.

For example, reducing discounts may improve contribution per order but still be a poor decision if it causes a larger decline in profitable orders. Similarly, reducing inventory may lower holding costs but hurt basket completion if important products become unavailable.

The aim is to improve sustainable contribution while protecting the customer experience.

## 8. Experiment Roadmap

The following experiments are proposed tests. No experiment has been conducted as part of this secondary-research teardown, and no results are claimed.

### Experiment 1: Promotion Incrementality Test

**Hypothesis:** Targeted promotions can reduce unnecessary discount expenditure without materially reducing incremental orders or customer retention.

**Design:**

1. Select eligible customers or geographic clusters with comparable historical purchasing patterns.
2. Establish a baseline for orders, discounts, contribution, and repeat purchase.
3. Assign eligible customers or clusters to a treatment group and a suitable control group.
4. Apply the proposed targeted-promotion strategy to the treatment group while maintaining the existing strategy for the control group.
5. Run the experiment long enough to observe immediate purchasing effects and short-term repeat behavior.
6. Compare results while accounting for customer mix, seasonality, and other relevant factors.

The analytics team should determine the appropriate sample size, experiment duration, and randomization approach. The design should minimize contamination between treatment and control groups.

**Primary success metric:** Incremental contribution per eligible customer over the experiment period.

**Supporting metrics:**
- Discount expenditure per completed order.
- Orders per eligible customer.
- Conversion rate.
- Repeat purchase rate.
- Average order value.

**Guardrail metrics:**
- Customer retention.
- Cancellation and refund rates.
- Customer complaints.
- Basket completion.
- Service quality.

**Decision rule:** Expand the intervention only if it produces a credible improvement in incremental contribution without an unacceptable deterioration in customer outcomes. If contribution declines or retention deteriorates materially, revise or stop the intervention.

#### Financial Impact Estimation

**Incremental contribution per eligible customer** = Average contribution per eligible customer in the treatment group − Average contribution per eligible customer in the control group.

**Estimated rollout benefit** = Measured incremental contribution per eligible customer × Eligible customers reached during rollout, adjusted for expected adoption and relevant differences between the experimental and rollout populations.

**Net benefit** = Estimated incremental contribution − Additional implementation and operating costs not already included in the contribution calculation.

The analysis must account for the experiment period and the eligible population. Results should not be extrapolated to the entire business without considering representativeness and rollout feasibility.

Improvements in contribution do not translate one-for-one into adjusted EBITDA because fixed operating expenses, corporate costs, depreciation, and other adjustments also matter.

### Experiment 2: Store Productivity Pilot

**Hypothesis:** Targeted operational changes can improve contribution in underperforming stores without reducing fulfilment reliability.

**Design:**
- Select comparable stores using factors such as maturity, demand patterns, and geography.
- Diagnose their cost and demand patterns before choosing an intervention.
- Test changes in staffing, replenishment, or local assortment in selected stores.
- Compare results with suitable control stores or a credible baseline.

**Success metrics:**
- Orders per store per day.
- Store-level contribution.
- Delivery cost per order.
- Product availability.
- On-time delivery and cancellation rates.

**Decision rule:** Expand the intervention if the measured financial improvement is credible and service-quality guardrails remain acceptable.

### Experiment 3: Priority-SKU Availability Pilot

**Hypothesis:** Better replenishment planning for frequently purchased essentials can improve availability and reduce lost sales.

**Design:**
- Select priority SKUs and participating stores using historical demand and stockout data.
- Test improved replenishment rules in selected stores.
- Compare availability, inventory turns, and contribution with a suitable baseline or control group.

**Success metrics:**
- Availability rate for priority SKUs.
- Stockout rate.
- Inventory turnover.
- Waste and shrinkage.
- Contribution from affected categories.

**Decision rule:** Expand only if better availability improves contribution without creating excessive inventory or waste.

### Experiment 4: Basket-Relevance Test

**Hypothesis:** Relevant product recommendations can increase basket contribution without reducing conversion or customer trust.

**Design:**
- Test contextual recommendations against a control group.
- Ensure suggested products are relevant to the customer's existing basket or stated needs.
- Evaluate incremental outcomes rather than attributing all purchases to the recommendation.

**Success metrics:**
- Incremental contribution per order.
- Conversion rate.
- Average order value.
- Repeat purchase.
- Customer complaints.

**Decision rule:** Retain the feature only if it creates incremental value without materially harming customer experience.

### Experiment Roadmap Summary

The promotion experiment is a practical starting point because its incremental effect can be measured through a controlled comparison. However, the final experiment should be selected after unit-economics diagnosis confirms that promotional spending is a material and actionable opportunity.

No experiment should be considered successful solely because it increases gross order value. The relevant outcome is incremental contribution with acceptable customer and operational guardrails.

## 9. Limitations and Open Questions

This analysis uses public reporting and strategic hypotheses. It does not have access to Instamart's internal store-level profit-and-loss statements, customer-level economics, delivery costs, supplier contracts, or controlled experiment results.

Consequently, the analysis cannot establish the precise causes of Instamart's adjusted EBITDA losses or quantify the financial impact of the proposed interventions.

### Open Questions

1. Which cost categories account for the largest share of Instamart's losses?
2. What is contribution per order by store, city, basket size, and customer cohort?
3. How much of the loss is associated with new-store expansion versus mature-store operations?
4. How much promotional spending generates incremental demand?
5. What are the economics of the inventory-led operating model after accounting for working capital and inventory risk?
6. How much of the performance gap with Blinkit reflects scale, business-model differences, assortment, pricing, operational efficiency, or other factors?
7. Which initiatives would deliver the largest sustainable improvement in contribution?

Answering these questions requires more granular company disclosures, internal operating data, and carefully designed experiments.

### Evidence Policy

- Company disclosures are the preferred sources for reported financial and operating figures.
- News articles provide additional context and independent reporting.
- Proposed customer problems, root causes, and interventions are treated as hypotheses unless supported by direct evidence.
- No internal company data, completed experiments, or unverified financial impact estimates are represented as facts.

## 10. Conclusion

Instamart's challenge is not simply that it operates fewer dark stores than Blinkit. The key question is whether its orders, stores, and customer relationships generate sufficient contribution to support the cost of the business.

The proposed strategy is to improve unit-economics visibility, raise dark-store productivity, make promotional spending more incremental, improve basket contribution, optimize inventory availability, and evaluate operating-model changes using measurable evidence.

The order of implementation should be determined by the largest verified sources of losses and the feasibility of improving them.

**Final recommendation:** Prioritize contribution-per-order analysis and store-level productivity diagnosis first. Use these findings to identify the highest-impact operational interventions, then test them with clear financial outcomes and customer-experience guardrails.

The goal is not to maximize short-term margin at any cost. It is to build a quick-commerce business that serves customers reliably while generating sustainable contribution and moving toward profitability.
