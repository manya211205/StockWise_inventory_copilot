# Swiggy Instamart Product Teardown: Improving Unit Economics and Building a Path to Profitability

## 1. Executive Summary

Swiggy Instamart operates in India's quick-commerce market, delivering groceries and everyday products through a network of dark stores.

In Q2 FY27, the quarter ended September 30, 2026, Instamart reported an adjusted EBITDA loss of ₹895 crore, while Blinkit reported adjusted EBITDA profit of ₹184 crore. Instamart operated 1,240 dark stores compared with Blinkit's 2,751.

These figures highlight a substantial difference in reported operating performance. However, store count alone cannot explain the gap. Differences in order volume, average order value, revenue per order, delivery costs, discounts, store maturity, assortment, and operating models may all contribute.

**Central product and business question:**

How can Swiggy Instamart improve contribution per order and dark-store productivity, reduce operating losses, and build a sustainable path to profitability without damaging customer retention or the convenience proposition?

This teardown uses public financial disclosures and industry reporting to develop a set of hypotheses and recommendations. The hypotheses require validation using internal operating data and customer research.

### 2.2 What the numbers suggest

**Observation 1: Instamart is growing, but its losses remain substantial.**

Instamart's NOV grew 34.8% year-on-year to ₹6,594 crore. Orders increased 29.3% to approximately 130 million, and monthly transacting users increased 23% to 14.8 million.

This indicates that the business is attracting customers and processing more orders, but growth has not yet translated into adjusted EBITDA profitability.

**Observation 2: Blinkit has substantially greater scale.**

Blinkit's larger store network, higher NOV, and faster growth may support greater purchasing leverage, denser delivery operations, and stronger utilization of fixed costs.

These are plausible mechanisms, not proof that scale alone explains its profitability.

**Observation 3: The next stage requires better unit economics, not growth at any cost.**

Increasing order volume can help absorb fixed costs, but only when incremental orders have sufficiently attractive economics. If orders generate negative contribution after variable costs, growth may increase losses rather than solve them.

## 3. Problem Statement

### 3.1 Primary problem statement

Swiggy Instamart has demonstrated meaningful customer and order growth, yet its quick-commerce business continues to generate substantial adjusted EBITDA losses.

The business needs to improve the economics of individual orders and dark stores while preserving the customer value proposition that drives purchase frequency and retention.

### 3.2 Business objective

Improve Instamart's contribution per order and store-level operating performance, thereby reducing adjusted EBITDA losses and establishing a credible path toward sustained profitability.

### 3.3 Constraints

The strategy should avoid:

- Reducing discounts indiscriminately if doing so materially harms retention.
- Increasing basket size through unnecessary purchases that damage customer trust.
- Expanding into locations without sufficient demand density.
- Cutting delivery or store costs in ways that materially reduce reliability.
- Adding assortment or private labels without validating demand, margin, and inventory risks.

The goal is profitable, durable growth—not profitability achieved at the expense of the core customer experience.

## 4. Root-Cause Analysis: Problems to Investigate

The following are potential causes of weak unit economics. Public disclosures identify relevant pressures, but they do not establish the precise contribution of each cause to Instamart's losses.

### Problem 1: Low contribution per order

**Hypothesis:** The revenue generated per order may not sufficiently cover variable fulfilment, delivery, discounting, payment, and other order-related costs.

Swiggy reported that revenue per order had increased by ₹25 and cost per order had decreased by ₹3 between Q4 FY25 and the period discussed in its May 2026 disclosure. It also reported improved contribution margins.

Source: [Swiggy's May 2026 financial results](https://www.swiggy.com/corporate/press-release/swiggys-food-delivery-gov-grew-22-6-yoy-with-inr-1-000-cr-in-adjusted-ebitda-overall-revenue-surges-45-to-inr-6383-cr/)

This indicates that order economics are a recognized improvement area, although the latest quarter's unit economics must be assessed separately.

**Data required:**
- Revenue per order.
- Discounts and promotional cost per order.
- Picking, packing, and delivery cost per order.
- Contribution margin by city, store, cohort, and basket size.

### Problem 2: Uneven dark-store productivity

**Hypothesis:** Some stores may not generate enough orders or gross profit to cover their operating costs.

A larger network can improve availability and coverage, but it also creates rent, labor, inventory, and other fixed or semi-fixed costs.

**Data required:**
- Orders per store per day.
- Gross profit and contribution per store.
- Store-level operating costs.
- Time taken for new stores to reach stable order volume.
- Store-level profitability distribution.

The appropriate response is not automatically to open more stores or close existing ones. It is to identify which locations have attractive demand and which require operational improvement.

### Problem 3: Demand density and delivery efficiency

**Hypothesis:** In some service areas, order density may be insufficient to support efficient delivery operations.

Low density can increase travel time per order, reduce the number of orders completed per delivery partner hour, and increase fulfilment cost.

**Data required:**
- Orders by delivery zone and time of day.
- Delivery cost per completed order.
- Orders per delivery partner hour.
- Average delivery distance and time.
- Cancellation and late-delivery rates.

### Problem 4: Customer acquisition and promotional spending

**Hypothesis:** Some customers may be disproportionately dependent on discounts, making their orders less profitable.

Promotions can help attract customers and increase purchase frequency. However, discounts that subsidize orders that would have occurred anyway may destroy margin without creating incremental value.

**Data required:**
- Discount cost per order and customer.
- Incremental orders attributable to promotions.
- Repeat purchase and retention by acquisition cohort.
- Contribution margin for discounted versus non-discounted orders.
- Customer lifetime contribution after acquisition costs.

### Problem 5: Basket economics and assortment

**Hypothesis:** A subset of baskets may be too small or have an unfavorable product mix to cover fulfilment costs.

Swiggy has discussed differentiated assortment, including its Noice private label, Nectr fresh produce offering, and Switch brand partnerships.

Source: [Mint: Instamart's Q2 FY27 performance and strategy](https://www.livemint.com/companies/company-results/swiggy-quick-commerce-test-instamart-losses-hit-895-crore-food-delivery-11792674493845.html)

Assortment expansion can create additional revenue opportunities, but low-velocity products may increase inventory holding costs, spoilage, and working-capital requirements.

**Data required:**
- Average order value and items per order.
- Gross margin by category and SKU.
- Inventory turnover and stockout rates.
- Waste, expiry, and shrinkage.
- Incremental basket contribution from cross-category purchases.

### Problem 6: Inventory ownership and working capital

**Hypothesis:** The operating model and inventory-management process may influence gross margin, stock availability, inventory losses, and working-capital efficiency.

Swiggy has discussed moving Instamart toward an inventory-led operating model and indicated that it expects this change to improve contribution margin when fully implemented.

Source: [Mint: Instamart's Q2 FY27 results](https://www.livemint.com/companies/company-results/swiggy-quick-commerce-test-instamart-losses-hit-895-crore-food-delivery-11792674493845.html)

The actual effect will depend on supplier terms, purchasing efficiency, inventory turns, product freshness, shrinkage, and execution.

**Data required:**
- Gross margin by category.
- Supplier terms and purchase costs.
- Inventory days and stock turnover.
- Waste and shrinkage rates.
- Stock availability and fulfilment rates.
- Working capital tied up in inventory.

## 5. Recommendations

The recommendations below are proposed interventions to test, not confirmed solutions.

### Recommendation 1: Build a contribution-per-order improvement program

**Priority: Very high**

Create a unified unit-economics view for each order, store, city, and customer cohort.

Track revenue and variable costs consistently so the business can identify where contribution is being lost.

**Actions:**
1. Break down revenue per order into product margin, delivery charges, platform fees, advertising, and other applicable revenue.
2. Track variable costs such as discounts, picking, packing, delivery, payment processing, and product losses.
3. Identify low-contribution order segments.
4. Run targeted interventions against the largest cost drivers.
5. Review contribution changes alongside retention and customer satisfaction.

**Why this matters:** It creates a measurable foundation for every other margin-improvement initiative.

### Recommendation 2: Improve dark-store productivity before accelerating expansion

**Priority: Very high**

Evaluate stores by maturity, demand density, contribution, and operating cost.

**Actions:**
- Segment stores into new, ramping, mature-profitable, and underperforming cohorts.
- Identify the reasons behind weak productivity in underperforming stores.
- Improve local assortment and inventory availability where demand supports it.
- Adjust staffing to observed order patterns while protecting service levels.
- Expand selectively in areas where expected demand and store-level economics justify the investment.

**Success measures:**
- Orders per store per day.
- Store-level contribution.
- Time to reach store-level contribution breakeven.
- Rent and labor cost per order.
- Store-level return on invested capital.

**Trade-off:** Slower expansion may sacrifice some market-share growth. However, opening stores without an attractive path to adequate utilization can increase operating losses.

### Recommendation 3: Make promotions incremental and contribution-aware

**Priority: Very high**

Move from broad discounting toward targeted promotions based on incremental customer value.

**Actions:**
- Test promotions among comparable customer groups.
- Measure incremental orders and contribution rather than total orders alone.
- Reduce subsidies for customers who purchase regularly without discounts, where evidence supports doing so.
- Use targeted offers to reactivate genuinely lapsed customers.
- Avoid abrupt discount withdrawal without measuring its effect on retention.

**Success measures:**
- Incremental contribution per promotion.
- Cost per incremental order.
- Repeat purchase after the promotion.
- Retention by acquisition cohort.
- Share of orders that remain contribution-positive after incentives.

**Trade-off:** Lower promotional spending may improve short-term margins but could reduce acquisition or purchase frequency. Experiments must measure both effects.

### Recommendation 4: Improve basket economics without compromising trust

**Priority: High**

Encourage customers to consolidate relevant purchases when doing so improves convenience and order economics.

**Possible experiments:**
- Relevant frequently bought together recommendations.
- Reminders for commonly replenished household essentials.
- Curated grocery lists based on user-selected needs.
- Contextual suggestions for complementary products.
- Clear communication of any minimum-order requirements or delivery charges.

Recommendations should be relevant rather than manipulative. Customers should not be encouraged to buy products they do not need.

**Success measures:**
- Incremental contribution per order.
- Average order value.
- Items per order.
- Conversion rate.
- Repeat purchase and customer complaints.

### Recommendation 5: Optimize assortment and inventory availability together

**Priority: High**

Use SKU-level demand and profitability data to determine what each store should stock.

**Actions:**
- Identify high-demand, high-contribution products that frequently go out of stock.
- Improve replenishment planning for important essentials.
- Reduce inventory exposure to low-velocity products where demand evidence supports the decision.
- Monitor expiry, spoilage, and shrinkage for relevant categories.
- Test category-specific assortment rather than using the same assortment everywhere.

**Success measures:**
- Product availability rate.
- Stockout rate for priority SKUs.
- Lost sales attributable to stockouts, where measurable.
- Inventory turnover.
- Waste and shrinkage.
- Gross profit after inventory losses.

**Trade-off:** Reducing assortment can lower inventory costs but may also reduce basket completion and customer satisfaction. Assortment changes should be tested at a store or category level.

### Recommendation 6: Improve delivery density and fulfilment efficiency

**Priority: High**

Investigate where delivery costs are elevated relative to order volume.

**Actions:**
- Analyze demand by geographic zone and time of day.
- Improve order allocation and dispatch where operationally feasible.
- Align staffing and delivery capacity with demand patterns.
- Evaluate delivery radii and service promises using actual cost and customer outcomes.
- Avoid expanding coverage in areas where demand cannot support efficient fulfilment.

**Success measures:**
- Delivery cost per order.
- Orders per delivery partner hour.
- On-time delivery rate.
- Cancellation rate.
- Customer complaints and repeat usage.

**Trade-off:** Narrower coverage or different service promises could reduce costs but may make the service less convenient. Reliability and accessibility should remain guardrails.

### Recommendation 7: Use private labels and differentiated assortment selectively

**Priority: Medium–high**

Instamart's existing private-label and differentiated-assortment initiatives provide a starting point for evaluating whether exclusive products can improve margins and customer differentiation.

**Actions:**
- Identify categories with repeat demand and attractive margin potential.
- Test a limited assortment before expanding.
- Compare contribution after sourcing, fulfilment, marketing, and inventory losses.
- Evaluate whether private-label purchases are incremental or replace profitable existing products.
- Monitor quality, complaints, and repeat purchase.

**Success measures:**
- Contribution margin by SKU.
- Repeat purchase rate.
- Inventory turnover.
- Waste and returns.
- Customer satisfaction and complaints.

**Trade-off:** Private labels may offer better product economics, but they introduce quality, brand, and inventory risks. Higher gross margin does not automatically mean higher net contribution.

### Recommendation 8: Evaluate store-level operating-model changes

**Priority: Medium–high**

Evaluate the economics of the inventory-led operating model discussed by Swiggy using controlled comparisons where feasible.

**Actions:**
- Compare comparable stores or categories operating under different models.
- Track purchase cost, product availability, inventory turns, and shrinkage.
- Measure changes in gross margin and contribution.
- Monitor working capital and operational complexity.
- Expand the model only when measured benefits exceed the additional costs and risks.

**Success measures:**
- Gross margin.
- Contribution margin.
- Inventory days.
- Stockout and fulfilment rates.
- Waste and shrinkage.
- Working-capital requirements.

**Trade-off:** Greater control over inventory and sourcing may improve economics but can increase inventory risk and working-capital needs.

## 8. Experiment Roadmap

### Experiment 1: Promotion Incrementality Test

**Hypothesis:** Targeted promotions can reduce discount expenditure without materially reducing incremental orders or retention.

Compare eligible customer groups under different promotion strategies.

Measure contribution after discounts, incremental order volume, repeat purchase, and retention.

### Experiment 2: Store Productivity Pilot

**Hypothesis:** Targeted operational changes can improve contribution in underperforming stores without reducing fulfilment reliability.

Select comparable stores, diagnose their cost and demand patterns, and test changes in staffing, replenishment, and local assortment.

Measure orders per store, contribution, delivery reliability, and stock availability.

### Experiment 3: Priority-SKU Availability Pilot

**Hypothesis:** Better replenishment planning for frequently purchased essentials can improve availability and reduce lost sales.

Select relevant SKUs and stores, compare against a suitable baseline or control group, and track availability, inventory turns, and contribution.

### Experiment 4: Basket-Relevance Test

**Hypothesis:** Relevant product recommendations can increase basket contribution without reducing conversion or customer trust.

Test contextual recommendations against a control group.

Measure incremental contribution per order, conversion, repeat purchase, and complaints.

No experiment should be considered successful solely because it increases gross order value. The relevant outcome is incremental contribution with acceptable customer and operational guardrails.

## 9. Limitations and Open Questions

This analysis uses public reporting and strategic hypotheses. It does not have access to Instamart's internal store-level profit-and-loss statements, customer-level economics, delivery costs, supplier contracts, or controlled experiment results.

The following questions remain unresolved:

1. Which cost categories account for the largest share of Instamart's losses?
2. What is contribution per order by store, city, basket size, and customer cohort?
3. How much of the loss is associated with new-store expansion versus mature-store operations?
4. How much promotional spending generates incremental demand?
5. What are the economics of the inventory-led operating model after accounting for working capital and inventory risk?
6. How much of the performance gap with Blinkit reflects scale, business-model differences, assortment, pricing, operational efficiency, or other factors?
7. Which initiatives would deliver the largest sustainable improvement in contribution?

Answering these questions requires more granular company disclosures, internal operating data, and carefully designed experiments.

## 10. Conclusion

Instamart's challenge is not simply that it operates fewer dark stores than Blinkit. The key question is whether its orders, stores, and customer relationships generate sufficient contribution to support the cost of the business.

The proposed strategy is to improve unit-economics visibility, raise dark-store productivity, make promotional spending more incremental, improve basket contribution, optimize inventory availability, and evaluate operating-model changes with measurable evidence.

The order of implementation should be determined by the largest verified sources of losses and the feasibility of improving them.

**Final recommendation:** Prioritize contribution-per-order analysis and store-level productivity diagnosis first. Use these findings to identify the highest-impact operational interventions, then test them with clear financial outcomes and customer-experience guardrails.

The goal is not to maximize short-term margin at any cost. It is to build a quick-commerce business that can serve customers reliably while generating sustainable contribution and moving toward profitability.

## 12. Step 4: Margin Improvement Strategy and Experiment Design

### 12.1 Objective

The objective of this stage is to translate the business diagnosis into a measurable intervention that could improve Swiggy Instamart's unit economics.

The strategy focuses on two levers:

1. Increasing revenue and contribution generated by each order.
2. Reducing fulfilment and operating costs without materially damaging customer experience.

The intervention will be evaluated using controlled experiments and financial metrics rather than assumptions about the causes of Instamart's losses.

### 12.2 What Management Has Already Disclosed

In its Q2 FY27 shareholder communication, Swiggy described its path toward Instamart's adjusted EBITDA breakeven.

Management indicated that it needs approximately 2.3 times its current scale and contribution of ₹30 per order. It expects around two-thirds of the improvement to come from higher revenue per order, supported by product mix, own brands, and advertising, with the remaining one-third coming from lower cost per order through densification, automation, and operating leverage.

**Source:** Swiggy Limited, Q2 FY27 shareholder letter, published October 2026.

This is management's stated direction, not an independently verified forecast of when profitability will be achieved.

### 12.3 Selected Opportunity: Improve Contribution per Order

**Problem hypothesis:**

Instamart may be able to improve its operating economics by increasing incremental contribution per order through better product mix, more efficient promotional spending, and lower fulfilment costs.

The available public disclosures do not establish which individual lever would produce the greatest incremental benefit. Therefore, the first step is to measure the current economics and identify the most promising intervention.

### 12.4 Proposed Intervention

I propose a contribution-led optimization program with three workstreams.

#### Workstream A: Improve Product Mix and Basket Contribution

Potential actions:
- Identify categories and products that generate attractive contribution after relevant variable costs.
- Test relevant cross-category recommendations for customers already building a basket.
- Evaluate private-label products where customer demand and product quality justify expansion.
- Improve availability of frequently purchased, economically attractive products.

The objective is not to maximize basket value indiscriminately. It is to increase incremental contribution while preserving customer trust and convenience.

#### Workstream B: Make Promotions More Incremental

Potential actions:
- Identify customer cohorts that respond to discounts with genuinely incremental purchases.
- Test targeted promotions against a suitable control group.
- Reduce unnecessary discount expenditure where the data shows that purchases would likely occur without the promotion.
- Monitor repeat purchase and retention after promotional changes.

Promotions should be evaluated on incremental contribution, not merely the number of orders generated.

#### Workstream C: Reduce Fulfilment Cost per Order

Potential actions:
- Analyze delivery cost and order density by location and time period.
- Identify opportunities to improve order allocation and fulfilment efficiency.
- Evaluate picking and packing processes for avoidable delays or labor costs.
- Use automation selectively where expected savings justify implementation costs.

Any efficiency improvement must preserve acceptable delivery reliability and product quality.

### 12.5 First Experiment: Promotion Incrementality Test

**Hypothesis**

A more targeted promotion strategy can reduce discount expenditure per order without materially reducing customer retention or incremental demand.

**Experiment design**

1. Select eligible customers or geographic clusters with comparable historical purchasing patterns.
2. Establish a baseline for orders, discounts, contribution, and repeat purchase.
3. Assign eligible customers or clusters to a test group and a suitable control group.
4. Apply the proposed targeted-promotion strategy to the test group while maintaining the existing strategy for the control group.
5. Run the experiment for a period long enough to observe both immediate purchasing effects and short-term repeat behavior.
6. Compare results after accounting for differences in customer mix, seasonality, and other relevant factors.

The experiment should be designed with the analytics team to avoid contamination between test and control groups.

**Primary success metric**

Incremental contribution per eligible customer over the experiment period.

**Supporting metrics**
- Discount expenditure per completed order.
- Orders per eligible customer.
- Conversion rate.
- Repeat purchase rate.
- Average order value.

**Guardrail metrics**
- Customer retention.
- Cancellation and refund rates.
- Customer complaints.
- Basket completion.
- Service quality.

**Decision rule**

Expand the intervention only if it produces a credible improvement in incremental contribution and does not cause an unacceptable deterioration in customer outcomes.

If contribution declines or customer retention deteriorates materially, revise or stop the intervention.

No result should be claimed until the experiment has actually been conducted.

### 12.6 Financial Impact Estimation

The potential impact should be estimated using measured changes rather than invented assumptions.

For a defined period:

**Incremental contribution = Incremental contribution per order × Eligible completed orders**

For a promotion experiment, calculate the difference in contribution between the treatment and control groups, adjusting for their eligible customer populations and the experiment period.

**Net experiment benefit = Incremental contribution − Incremental implementation costs**

Where applicable, also account for ongoing technology, operational, and campaign costs that are not already included in contribution.

The analysis should distinguish incremental contribution from adjusted EBITDA. Improvements in order contribution may not translate one-for-one into adjusted EBITDA because fixed operating expenses, corporate costs, depreciation, and other adjustments also matter.

A company-wide impact estimate should only be produced after the treatment effect, eligible population, rollout feasibility, and potential side effects have been measured.

### 12.7 Metrics Framework

**Primary business metric:** Contribution per order.

**Supporting business metrics:**
- Contribution margin as a percentage of gross order value.
- Revenue per order.
- Variable cost per order.
- Discount expenditure per order.
- Store-level contribution.

**Customer metrics:**
- Repeat purchase rate.
- Order frequency.

**Guardrail metrics:**
- Cancellation rate.

These metrics should be interpreted together. For example, reducing discounts may improve contribution per order but still be a poor decision if it causes a larger decline in profitable orders.

### 12.8 Risks and Trade-offs

**Risk 1: Reduced discounts lower demand**

Mitigation: Use controlled experiments and evaluate incremental contribution alongside retention.

**Risk 2: Promoting higher-margin products reduces customer relevance**

Mitigation: Prioritize relevance, quality, and customer preferences over margin alone.

**Risk 3: Delivery-cost reductions damage reliability**

Mitigation: Monitor service quality as a guardrail and stop changes that materially harm customer outcomes.

**Risk 4: Higher basket values increase inventory complexity**

Mitigation: Evaluate SKU-level demand, stock availability, inventory turns, and product losses.

**Risk 5: Improvements do not scale uniformly**

Mitigation: Test across representative stores and customer cohorts before broad rollout.

### 12.9 Limitations

This is a proposed experiment, not a completed business intervention.

The analysis does not have access to Instamart's internal promotion-level data, customer-level contribution, fulfilment cost breakdowns, or experimental results. Consequently, no numerical impact estimate or expected percentage improvement is claimed.

The promotion experiment is a practical starting point, but internal unit-economics data may identify a higher-impact opportunity in product mix, delivery efficiency, or another cost category.

### 12.10 Conclusion

Instamart's margin-improvement strategy should focus on measurable improvements in contribution per order and operating efficiency.

A controlled promotion incrementality experiment provides one way to test whether discount expenditure can be reduced without sacrificing profitable demand or customer retention.

The final intervention should be selected based on observed contribution drivers, validated customer behavior, and experimental evidence. Sustainable profitability requires both sound unit economics and a reliable customer experience.
